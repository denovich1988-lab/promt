Benchmarking turns "competitors are doing better" into something you can act on: specific criteria, scored against evidence, with clear gaps. Without a structure it becomes a feature list or a pile of screenshots. This competitive benchmarking prompt sets weighted criteria, scores each company with the evidence behind it, separates facts from assumptions, and ends with the few gaps worth closing first.

## Prompt

```prompt
Act as a strategy analyst. Build a competitive benchmarking report.

Our company: [NAME, PRODUCT, TARGET CUSTOMER]
Competitors to benchmark: [3–6 NAMES]
What we want to learn: [E.G. WHY WE LOSE DEALS, PRICING POSITION, CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE, DIGITAL PRESENCE]
Benchmark areas: [E.G. PRICE, FEATURES, ONBOARDING, SUPPORT, DELIVERY SPEED, REVIEWS, CONTENT, HIRING]
Evidence I have: [PASTE NOTES, PRICING PAGES, REVIEW THEMES, MYSTERY-SHOP RESULTS, WIN/LOSS NOTES]

Deliver:
1. Benchmark criteria (8–12), each with a definition, how to measure it and a weight that adds up to 100%, explained by what customers value.
2. A scorecard table: criteria × companies, scores 1–5, weighted totals.
3. For every score, the evidence it's based on, marked as fact (from my evidence), estimate, or unknown. Don't invent competitor data; list unknowns as research tasks.
4. Where each competitor is clearly strongest and weakest.
5. Our top 3 gaps and top 3 advantages.
6. Recommended moves: quick wins (under 1 month), medium-term, and things not worth copying.
7. A plan to repeat the benchmark quarterly: data sources and who collects what.
```

## How to use it

1. **Collect evidence first**: pricing pages, trial sign-ups, support tests and review themes.
2. **Agree the weights with your team** before scoring, so the results aren't argued away later.
3. **Fill the unknowns** with the research tasks the prompt lists, then rerun the scorecard.

## Tips

- Benchmark against the competitors you actually lose deals to, not only the biggest names.
- Re-check competitor pricing and features before sharing; they change often.
