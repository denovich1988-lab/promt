Officials and executives rarely read full research reports; they read two-page briefs. A good policy brief states the problem plainly, summarises the strongest evidence, compares realistic options and ends with recommendations someone can act on. This policy brief prompt turns your findings and sources into that structure without overstating what the evidence shows.

## Prompt

```prompt
Act as a policy analyst. Write a policy brief from my research.

Issue: [THE PROBLEM IN ONE SENTENCE]
Audience: [E.G. CITY COUNCIL, MINISTRY OFFICIALS, HOSPITAL BOARD]
Jurisdiction and context: [WHERE, CURRENT RULES OR PROGRAMMES]
Key findings and sources: [PASTE FINDINGS WITH CITATIONS]
Options being considered: [LIST, OR "SUGGEST OPTIONS"]
Constraints: [BUDGET, POLITICAL, LEGAL, TIMING]
Length: [E.G. 2 PAGES / 1,000 WORDS]

Write the brief with:
1. Title and a 3-sentence executive summary with the main recommendation.
2. The problem: scale, who is affected, why it matters now — using only my figures.
3. What the evidence shows, noting strength and gaps.
4. Policy options (2–4): description, expected impact, cost, feasibility, risks, in a comparison table.
5. Recommendations: specific, actionable, in priority order, with who should act.
6. Implementation and monitoring: first steps and indicators to track.
7. Sources list in my citation style.
Use plain language, no jargon. Where evidence is weak or mixed, say so. Mark claims needing a source with {CITE}.
```

## How to use it

1. **Paste findings with their sources** so every claim in the brief can be traced.
2. **Name the real audience**; a council brief and a ministry brief need different detail.
3. **Ask a colleague outside the field** to read it; if they can't explain the recommendation back, simplify.

## Tips

- Put the recommendation on page one; many readers stop there.
- Be honest about trade-offs; decision-makers trust briefs that show the costs too.
