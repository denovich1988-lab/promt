A strategy says where a business wants to go; the operating model says how the work actually gets done to get there: processes, people and roles, technology, data, governance and locations. A target operating model (TOM) compares today's setup with what the strategy needs and plans the moves between them. This target operating model prompt walks through the standard layers, finds the gaps and builds a phased roadmap.

## Prompt

```prompt
Act as a management consultant specialising in operating model design. Design a target operating model.

Organisation / unit: [NAME, WHAT IT DOES, SIZE, LOCATIONS]
Strategy and goals for the next 2–3 years: [E.G. DOUBLE ONLINE SALES, CUT COST TO SERVE BY 20%, ENTER NEW MARKET]
Current state: [HOW WORK IS DONE TODAY: KEY PROCESSES, TEAMS, SYSTEMS, PAIN POINTS]
Customers and channels: [WHO WE SERVE AND HOW]
Constraints: [BUDGET, REGULATION, UNIONS, LEGACY SYSTEMS, TIMELINE]

Deliver:
1. Design principles (5–7) that follow from the strategy.
2. For each layer, current state vs. target state and the gap: (a) processes and value chain, (b) organisation, roles and spans of control, (c) people and skills, (d) technology, (e) data and reporting, (f) governance and decision rights, (g) locations and sourcing (in-house, shared service, outsourced), (h) performance measures.
3. Two or three alternative models (e.g. centralised, federated, hub-and-spoke) with pros, cons and fit with the strategy, then a recommendation.
4. A phased roadmap (quick wins, 6–12 months, 12–24 months) with dependencies.
5. Main risks, change management needs and how to measure success.
Mark every assumption and the information I'd need to confirm it.
```

## How to use it

1. **Describe the current state honestly**, including workarounds; that's where most gaps are.
2. **Test the alternative models** with leaders before choosing one.
3. **Turn the roadmap into a project plan** with owners and budget once the model is agreed.

## Tips

- Start from the strategy and customer, not the org chart; structure follows the work.
- Decision rights (who decides what) often cause more friction than reporting lines.
