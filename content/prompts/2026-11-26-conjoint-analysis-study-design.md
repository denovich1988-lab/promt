Asking customers "which features matter?" gets you "all of them". A conjoint analysis survey makes people choose between realistic product bundles, revealing what they really value and how price changes their choice. This prompt helps you design the study: attributes and levels, choice tasks, sample size reasoning, screening questions and how to analyse and present the results.

## Prompt

```prompt
Act as a quantitative market researcher. Help me design a choice-based conjoint analysis study.

Product or service: [WHAT IT IS]
Decision I need to make: [E.G. PRICE POINT, WHICH FEATURES GO IN WHICH PLAN]
Target respondents: [WHO, HOW I'LL REACH THEM]
Attributes I'm considering: [E.G. PRICE, STORAGE, SUPPORT LEVEL, CONTRACT LENGTH, BRAND]
Survey tool: [E.G. CONJOINTLY, SAWTOOTH, QUALTRICS, SPRIG]
Budget or sample I can get: [NUMBER OF RESPONDENTS]

Create:
1. A final list of 4–6 attributes with 2–5 levels each; levels must be realistic, mutually exclusive and clearly worded. Explain any I should drop or merge.
2. Price levels that span a realistic range, with my reasoning.
3. The choice task setup: profiles per task, number of tasks, whether to include a "none" option, and why.
4. A sample size rationale (and the trade-off if I have fewer respondents), stated as a rule of thumb to confirm with the tool.
5. Screening and profiling questions, plus attention checks.
6. An example of 2 choice tasks as respondents would see them.
7. Analysis plan: part-worth utilities, attribute importance, willingness to pay, market simulator scenarios, segment comparisons.
8. Pitfalls: too many attributes, unrealistic combinations, mixing features customers don't understand.

Don't fabricate results; give placeholders where outputs would appear.
```

## How to use it

1. **Interview 5–10 customers first** so attribute names match how they talk.
2. **Pilot the survey** with a small group and check completion time and confusing wording.
3. **Use the simulator scenarios** to test the actual plans you're choosing between.

## Tips

- Keep attributes to what you can actually change; testing impossible options wastes respondents' attention.
- Synthetic AI respondents are no substitute for real customers in a pricing study.
